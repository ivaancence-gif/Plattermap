The hardest part of family dinner isn't the cooking — it's deciding. By Thursday, "what's for dinner?" has been asked four times and answered with a sigh. Meal planning fixes this, but most systems fail because they're built for people with more time, not less. Here's the one that sticks.

## The 30-minute Sunday system

### Step 1: Pick five, not seven (10 minutes)

Plan five dinners, not seven. Life will hand you leftovers, a late meeting, or a pizza night anyway. Choose:

- **Two familiar favorites** — meals you could cook half-asleep, like [Chicken Stir Fry](/recipe/8) or [Beef Tacos](/recipe/7)
- **Two easy-but-different** — same effort, new flavors: try [Salmon Teriyaki](/recipe/10) or [Chana Masala](/recipe/108)
- **One small adventure** — a weekend dish with more steps, like [Margherita Pizza](/recipe/9) or a slow-simmered [Lamb Curry](/recipe/11)

### Step 2: Balance the week's rhythm (5 minutes)

Match effort to energy. Monday gets a 20-minute meal. Friday gets assembly-only. The adventure waits for Saturday. A quick scan of cook times — every PlatterMap recipe shows one — makes this a sorting exercise, not a research project.

### Step 3: One consolidated shopping list (10 minutes)

Go recipe by recipe and merge ingredients into one list, grouped by store section: produce, dairy, pantry, meat. Two onions across three recipes means one line: "onions ×2." This single act kills both mid-week store runs and the wilted-spinach graveyard in your crisper.

### Step 4: Post the plan where everyone sees it (5 minutes)

Fridge, family chat, whiteboard — anywhere public. Two things happen: the "what's for dinner" question answers itself, and kids who know Tuesday is taco night arrive at the table already sold.

## Why this beats the fancy systems

Meal planning fails when it demands perfection. This system plans five dinners in half an hour and treats deviation as normal. The win isn't a beautiful calendar — it's Wednesday-you never staring into the fridge at 6pm.
